                                           Alignment Stabilizes LLM Reinforcement Learning

                                                                                            Abstract
                                              Reinforcement learning for large language models often uses separate systems for inference and
                                              training. Even with identical weights and BF16 tensors, implementation differences can make
                                              an intended on-policy update off-policy and destabilize training. Existing remedies align the
                                              entire inference and training stacks, change global precision, or modify the learning algorithm.
                                              We ask whether aligning selected parts of the execution stack can stabilize BF16 RL while other
                                              implementation differences remain. We align training-side rounding locations with inference in
                                              three regions—RoPE, SwiGLU, and residual RMSNorm—by retaining selected intermediates in
                                              FP32 until their BF16 outputs. Across four Qwen-family models, aligning all three regions prevents
                                              the collapse observed under the default BF16 baseline; RoPE-path alignment alone also suffices in
                                              the studied runs, despite remaining numerical discrepancies. The interventions keep the inference
                                              engine, matrix-multiplication and attention kernels, and learning objective unchanged.

                                         1   Introduction
                                         Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central method for post-training large language models (LLMs),
                                         improving mathematical reasoning, code generation, and search-assisted problem solving [5, 10, 25]. Main-
                                         taining stable optimization remains difficult, however, and model performance can collapse during training
                                         [37, 42]. Training–inference mismatch is one source of this instability [36, 43].
                                         Many RL systems use an inference engine such as vLLM to generate responses and a separate implementation,
                                         such as Hugging Face Transformers, to recompute their probabilities for training [14, 32]. Even with the same
                                         checkpoint and nominal data type, implementation differences can make the recomputed probabilities disagree
                                         with those of the policy that generated the responses. This discrepancy turns an intended on-policy update
                                         off-policy and can destabilize training [22, 36, 43].
                                         Prior studies stabilize training under this mismatch through global precision changes [22] or algorithmic
                                         corrections [36, 43]. Another approach seeks exact numerical agreement by aligning arithmetic and reduction
                                         order across engines [6, 40], including the precision of intermediate computations in RoPE and SwiGLU [20].
                                         We ask whether aligning only selected parts of the execution stack can stabilize BF16 RL while retaining the
                                         learning objective and allowing other implementation differences to remain.
                                         A compiled/eager comparison motivates this question. Both compiled and eager execution support stable


                                                                                                1
training when inference and training use aligned modes; the two crossed conditions collapse. This observation
directs attention to agreement between the engines and motivates inspecting the numerical changes introduced
by fusion. Separate kernels store some intermediates in BF16, whereas a fused kernel can retain them in FP32
until its output. We call this difference in where values are rounded a rounding-location mismatch.
Guided by graph inspection, we align training-side rounding locations with those in inference in three regions:
the RoPE path [28], SwiGLU [27], and residual RMSNorm [38]. We retain selected training intermediates
in FP32 and round each region’s outputs to BF16, preserving BF16 model weights, the inference engine,
matrix-multiplication and attention kernels, and learning objective.

Contribution. We show that selective rounding-location alignment can stabilize BF16 LLM RL without
full-stack numerical agreement or additional mismatch corrections in the learning objective. Across four
Qwen-family models, aligning all three regions prevents the collapse observed under the default BF16 baseline
despite numerical disagreement; RoPE-path alignment alone also suffices in every studied run. These results
establish selective alignment as a practical route to stable training in the studied settings.

2   Related Work
Corrections for training–inference mismatch. Truncated importance sampling and sequence-level trust-region
masking limit the effect of distribution shift between inference and training policies [15, 36]. Zheng et al.
[42] analyze how numerical disagreement and policy staleness affect token-level surrogate objectives. Other
remedies reduce the learning rate when response length signals impending instability [39], or accept candidate
updates using an inference-side discrepancy proxy [16].

Execution alignment. Batch-invariant kernels fix reduction structure to remove numerical dependence on
batch size [6]; invariant reductions can also extend agreement across tensor-parallel sizes [40]. Zhong et al.
[43] use the same Hugging Face implementation for inference and training with batch-invariant kernels. Their
experiments show that numerical disagreement alone can cause training collapse. In mixture-of-experts models,
replaying the expert selections made during inference reduces routing disagreement and stabilizes training [18].
The Ring-linear report aligns training and inference module by module [17]; Panda [20] explicitly matches
rounding locations in RoPE and SwiGLU as part of achieving bitwise-identical token log probabilities.

Precision and quantization. Qi et al. [22] show that switching from BF16 to FP16 reduces training–inference
mismatch and improves RL stability in their experiments. Low-bit RL systems also address the tradeoff
between inference efficiency and numerical agreement. Huang et al. [8] combine NVFP4 quantization with
low-rank adaptation to accelerate rollouts and study quantization noise as a source of exploration. Other
approaches combine FP8 linear-layer computation and KV-cache quantization with importance-sampling
corrections [23], or align the FP8 precision flow across training and rollouts to reduce numerical mismatch [33].
QaRL aligns low-bit matrix multiplications in the training forward pass with quantized inference, together
with importance correction and a modified policy objective [4]. Related work on long-context training shows
that BF16 errors can disrupt the relative-position property of rotary position embedding (RoPE) [31].

3   Training-Inference Mismatch in LLM RL
LLM RL commonly separates response generation from optimization. An inference engine such as vLLM
generates responses token by token, using an implementation optimized for efficient generation. A training
implementation such as Hugging Face Transformers then processes those same responses to recompute
their token probabilities and differentiate the learning objective [14, 32]. At identical weights, these two
implementations are intended to represent the same policy. Numerical differences can make their probabilities
disagree before any optimization step, creating training–inference mismatch in addition to the policy changes
caused by learning. This mismatch poses a major challenge for LLM RL, as it can destabilize training and
cause performance collapse [22, 36, 43].

3.1   Where the mismatch comes from
The same model can produce different floating-point results in different implementations. We distinguish
differences in precision and casts, kernels, and reduction order.

Precision and casts. Parameter, buffer, computation, and accumulation precision can differ, as can cast
locations. To accelerate generation, some inference engines quantize the KV cache or use low-precision
computation, reducing memory traffic or arithmetic cost; these choices can introduce further disagreement
with higher-precision training [23, 33]. BF16 tensor storage also does not imply that every intermediate is
computed in BF16. For two operations f and g, let Rbf16 denote rounding to BF16. Rounding the intermediate
and retaining it in FP32 give, respectively,

                           Rbf16 (g(Rbf16 (f (x))))        and               Rbf16 (g(f (x)))           .
                           |         {z           }                          |      {z      }
                         intermediate rounded to BF16                   intermediate retained in FP32

Both return BF16 outputs, but g can receive different values. We call this a rounding-location mismatch.
Compilation can introduce this difference through kernel fusion, which combines operations in one kernel: a
fused kernel can retain an intermediate in FP32, whereas separate kernels may store and reload it in BF16 [1].

Kernels. The same operation can use different kernels or decompositions. vLLM combines Q, K, and V
projections and gate and up projections, whereas Hugging Face may execute them separately; the systems
can also use different attention implementations [2].

Reduction order. Floating-point addition is not associative, so batch size, tensor parallelism, and kernel tiling
can change a reduction’s result. Invariant kernels across batch and parallel sizes fix the reduction structure.

5     Qwen Analysis: Where Fusion Moves Rounding Locations
We identify rounding-location mismatches by inspecting intermediate data types and fused operations in the
inference and training graphs. The aim is to find values stored in BF16 in one implementation but retained in

FP32 until a later operation in the other.
For Qwen2 and Qwen3 [34, 35], inspection of the compiled and eager vLLM paths identifies three regions
in which fusion changes rounding locations: Q/K normalization, when present, plus RoPE; SwiGLU’s
sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activation [3] and gate multiplication; and residual addition plus root mean square
normalization (RMSNorm).

5.1       The three affected regions
Q/K normalization and RoPE. In variants with Q/K normalization, fusion retains its statistics, normalized
and scaled Q/K values, and subsequent RoPE products and sums in FP32. Variants without Q/K normalization
still fuse RoPE products and sums. Only rotated Q/K outputs are stored in BF16.

SwiGLU. Fusion retains sigmoid and SiLU results in FP32 through multiplication with the up projection,
then stores the product in BF16. Eager execution stores the SiLU output in BF16 before multiplication.

Residual RMSNorm. Under fusion, RMSNorm consumes the unrounded FP32 residual sum and retains
its intermediate computations in FP32. Both its output and a separate copy of the sum for the continuing
residual stream are stored in BF16. Eager execution rounds the sum before RMSNorm. This region follows
both attention and MLP in every decoder block.
The intervention retains the identified training intermediates in FP32 and casts each region output back
to BF16. Changes are confined to pointwise and normalization operations in the RoPE, SwiGLU, and
residual-RMSNorm regions, preserving the inference engine and the compute-intensive matrix-multiplication
and attention kernels. Aligning all three regions adds 5.33–10.92% to forward-and-backward latency, including
communication, and 0.41–0.89 GiB of peak allocated memory per GPU across three benchmarked models
(Section C). The additional training time is expected to represent a smaller percentage of total RL time once
rollout generation is included.

8                              Structure of BF16 Rounding Phases
We explore the numerical structure of intermediates at the identified rounding locations. This characterization
complements the policy-discrepancy and RL experiments by examining the values directly affected by rounding.
A value’s BF16 cell phase is its normalized position between adjacent BF16 values; away from midpoint ties,
this position determines the direction and relative size of rounding error. Using the same three models as
in Section 6, we compare RoPE products, SiLU outputs, and residual sums with projection, attention, and
RMSNorm outputs.
For each signal s and layer ℓ, the phase-concentration statistic Aℓ,s is the root mean square of the first 16
Fourier magnitudes of the phase distribution; larger values indicate stronger departures from uniformity over
these frequencies. We report its median across layers, Abs . Section A.4 gives the sampling procedure, formal
definitions, and individual frequency spectra.
Figure 4 compares the groups. Across models, A  bs is 1.5 × 10−5 –8.0 × 10−4 for projection, attention, and
RMSNorm outputs, compared with 0.038–0.040 for SiLU, 0.054–0.192 for RoPE products, and 0.443–0.472 for
residual sums. The three groups whose rounding locations change under fusion have the largest median phase
concentrations. Some Q/K/V and attention layers have large maxima, but low medians and quartiles show
that they are isolated.
The three regions identified by graph inspection thus exhibit distinctive BF16 cell-phase structure across
all three models. The individual spectra also show consistent frequency patterns (Section A.4). These
measurements quantify how values are distributed within BF16 intervals; their rounding errors and downstream
effects additionally depend on interval widths and subsequent computations (Section A.5). The Fourier analysis
provides a complementary view of the affected intermediates alongside the static discrepancy reductions and
RL stabilization reported in Sections 6 and 7.

9                              Discussion
Why a small gap matters. Aligning all three regions reduces the static per-token KL by 9.1–25.5%, yet
prevents collapse in the RL runs. One possible explanation is that numerical errors at different locations
affect training updates differently. Aligning a sensitive region may therefore improve stability substantially
while leaving much of the average policy discrepancy intact.



                                                                                       8
Why not simply compile training? Compilation is a possible remedy: the compiled–compiled condition is
stable in the motivating experiment. It does, however, introduce practical costs. Hugging Face training
compilation covers both forward and backward passes and incurs a substantial initial compilation cost [9].
Variable response lengths can trigger recompilation, while data-dependent control flow and unsupported
operations can cause graph breaks [21]. Distributed sharding and custom kernels may also require compiler-
specific integration. A reported large-scale training deployment required explicit work on graph breaks, custom
kernels, and numerical agreement with eager execution [29]. Compiling Hugging Face also does not make it
the same implementation as compiled vLLM: precision and casts, kernels, and reduction order can still differ.

Alignment or increased training-side precision? With the eager training implementation and its precision
held fixed, switching inference from compiled to eager execution restores stability (Section 4). Together with
the crossed execution-mode controls, this supports execution alignment as a stabilizing factor and shows
that stability can be restored without increasing training-side precision. Section 6 also shows that higher
intermediate precision does not automatically reduce static policy discrepancy. Together with the targeted
rounding-location interventions, these results support rounding-location alignment as a stabilizing mechanism.

Limitations. The experiments cover two math datasets and models from a single architectural family.
Extending the intervention to other architectures requires inspecting their inference and training graphs to
identify the relevant rounding locations and adapt the alignment accordingly. The results demonstrate the
importance of rounding-location alignment for stable LLM RL; broader experiments are needed to establish
when it is sufficient across architectures and training settings.

Takeaway. The results support inspecting the inference and training graphs to identify and align rounding
locations moved by fusion as a targeted intervention for stabilizing BF16 RL. RoPE-path alignment alone
suffices to stabilize the studied BF16 RL runs. Aligning all three regions stabilizes training and gives the
lowest per-token KL among the conditions compared in Section 6. The interventions keep the inference engine,
matrix-multiplication and attention kernels, and training objective unchanged. Stable training therefore does
not require full-stack alignment in these experiments.

D    Implementation of the Zero-Mismatch Backend
The Zero-mismatch backend in Section 7 uses the same uncompiled Hugging Face forward implementation
for inference and training, preserving BF16 rounding locations. We implement batch-invariant kernels in
Triton [30] for matrix multiplication, log-softmax, mean reduction, and attention. Our implementation is
based on the code released by He and Thinking Machines Lab [6].
The matrix and reduction kernels keep accumulation order independent of batch size; log-softmax computes
log probabilities in FP32, and mean reduction covers normalization statistics. Inference and training also share
the same attention kernel and key/value traversal order, with FP32 accumulation and BF16 outputs. Cached
decoding retains this reduction scheme without separately reducing partitions of the key/value sequence,
aligning attention arithmetic with full-sequence recomputation.
