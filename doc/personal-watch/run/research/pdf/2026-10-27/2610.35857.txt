                                                     Do vision models encode the transition 2→3 the same way whether it appears in
                                                     handwritten digits or in hand gestures, and do they “understand” that clean→dirty
                                                     is the same transformation regardless of whether it is applied to a kitchen or a
                                                     carpet? The linear representation hypothesis (LRH) answers this for language
                                                     models: concept changes correspond to directions in (language) embedding space.
                                                     In vision, the same concept can manifest in visually unrelated regimes that rarely
                                                     co-occur: images of digits and hands do not share much in common in pixel
                                                     space. To understand whether vision models still encode the underlying abstract
                                                     concepts, we study 46 vision encoders across architectures, training objectives,
                                                     and scales, on 10 concept families including numerosity, age, object identity,
                                                     and MIT-States attributes. Within each regime, we find statistically significant
                                                     concept directions near-universally across our tested models. However, two distinct
                                                     regimes do not always share the same concept direction. Several models learn
                                                     multiple linear structures for the same concept transition, split across regimes. We
                                                     call this phenomenon fragmented linearity: strong local linearity coexisting with
                                                     near-absent global linearity. Fragmentation is, however, not fixed: models with
                                                     stronger local linearity progressively escape it (local-global linearity correlated
                                                     with τ ∈ [0.38, 0.89] across all concepts), and where global linearity does emerge,
                                                     concept directions can transfer as linear probes across regimes.


                                         1    Introduction
                                         The linear representation hypothesis (LRH) [1, 2] claims that the embedding spaces of large language
                                         models (LLMs) contain linear directions corresponding to high-level concept changes, enabling
                                         interpretability and algebraic interventions in representation space. Yet as vision encoders increasingly
                                         serve as the perceptual backbone of multimodal systems [3, 4], understanding whether the same
                                         linear structure holds in vision is essential to completing the picture.
                                         Vision is fundamentally different from language in that the same concept often manifests in visually
                                         drastically different ways. For instance, the concept of “3” can be represented using handwritten
                                         digits [5], the number of objects in an image [6] or hand gestures [7]. While these different groups
                                         (regimes) of images do not share much in common in pixel space, a human would understand that
                                         the same underlying concept is being manipulated; do vision embedding spaces encode the same
                                         abstractions? A linear representation in embedding space would hint that they do.
                                         Linear structure (linearity) in the embedding space is indeed often an assumption (implicit or
                                         otherwise) in prior works that attempt to interpret vision embeddings [8–10]. Prior work has also
                                         studied linearity of vision embeddings after projection onto a tangent space [11], with respect to
                                         ordinal attributes [12], and its emergence as a consequence of data diversity [13]. While these findings
                                         hint toward the presence of linear structure in vision embeddings, a more rigorous study exploring
                                         whether it survives across drastically unrelated visual regimes remains missing.
                                         To address this gap, we study 46 vision encoders varying in architecture, training objective, model
                                         size and pretraining data, using the concepts of numerosity, age, and object identity, as well as 7

2       Preliminaries and related work

We review three different notions of linear structure that appear in prior work and situate our study
within the existing landscape. Of these, the LRH is the main notion studied in our work.
Notation. We assume a concept space Z = Z1 × Z2 × · · · × Zk where the ith concept can take
one of | Zi | values; samples x ∈ X are generated as x = g(z), z ∈ Z where X is the input space
(images or text prompts). An (image or text) encoder is a function f : X → RD . Where convenient,
we will use the notation ϕ(z) := f (g(z)) for some z ∈ Z.

2.1      Different notions of linearity

(1) Linear separability. Linear probes [15] reveal whether different classes of a | Zi |-valued
concept can be separated linearly using a classifier h : RD → [0, 1]|Zi | . This notion of linearity
has been largely shown to hold for many modern models [16–21]; however, separability does not
guarantee that concepts admit consistent linear directions conducive to manipulation.

2.2   Related work

Interpretability and the linearity assumption. Early interpretability research pioneered by [8]
assumes local linear separability of samples belonging to a concept from those not belonging to
it. The assumption of concepts lying in some low-dimensional subspace is implicit [23] but not
otherwise verified. More recent works [24, 9, 10] train SAEs to sparsely decompose embeddings.
Nevertheless, it is unclear whether SAEs recover the “true” concept directions [25]. While the goal in
these works is interpretability of vision models, we focus on characterizing the geometry that enables
interpretability.
Linear structure in vision models. [26] discover task-aligned subspaces in CLIP’s image encoder.
Extending the findings from [22] to the vision domain, [11] show that CLIP image embeddings admit
approximately linear structure after projection onto the tangent space. [12] show that many ordinal
attributes can be linearly decoded from popular vision encoders, while [13] show compositional
linearity emerges as a consequence of training data diversity.
The above works assess linearity as a single global property of the representation. In contrast, we
consider visually different regimes separately and hypothesize that (1) individual regimes contain
strong concept representations, and (2) linearity (Definition 2.1) often holds only weakly because
regime-specific concept representations fail to transfer strongly. This is also akin to learning different
mechanisms for solving the same task in different contexts [27], although mechanistic interpretability
is outside our scope.
VLM embedding spaces. A broad body of prior work studies different aspects of VLM embedding
spaces in general. Of particular note have been the modality gap [28–30], binding capabilities [31–33]
and negation [34]. [35] propose geometric constraints for an “ideal” CLIP. In contrast to these works,
our work explores the local geometry of general vision models, including CLIP.
Compositional generalization and compositional zero-shot learning. VLMs have been frequently
shown to be suitable for compositional zero-shot learning [36–38], compositional retrieval [39–41],
and concept editing [42]. Other work has revealed shortcomings [43]. We take a complementary
approach and analyze the geometry of existing vision encoders for local linearity; our findings may
contribute towards explaining the compositional failures of VLMs.


                                                    3
Representation convergence. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis [44] argues that learned
representations are converging toward a shared, canonical model of the world. In contrast, we study a
different form of convergence: whether a single model’s embedding space aligns the same concept
across different regimes. Our perspective is closer to work questioning whether neural networks reuse
identical computational structures across settings [45, 27, 46]. In contrast, however, we focus on the
final-layer embeddings which are directly exposed to downstream tasks like zero-shot retrieval and
classification.

3     Fragmented Linearity

This section defines local linearity and fragmentation and lays out the measurements used throughout
the paper.

3.1     Regimes and approximate counterfactuals

Our linearity metric follows the LRH definition in Definition 2.1.

3.1.1    Regimes and local linearity

No comparable explicit signal links an image of the digit 3 to an image of three raised fingers.

old buildings do not. This motivates studying concept structure in vision at the level of data subsets
(digits, fingers, spheres, coins, buildings,Sand so on), which we call regimes. Formally, we use a

4   Results
We evaluate 46 vision models spanning contrastive, self-supervised, and supervised training objec-
tives, on 10 concepts (numerosity, age, object identity, and seven MIT-States attributes). For each
concept, images are partitioned into regimes (e.g., MNIST digits, raised fingers, rendered spheres
for numerosity). For each model-concept pair, we compute per-regime-pair DC, yielding regime ×
regime matrices (Figure 2). Each entry in such a matrix is a measure of local linearity within that
regime (diagonal entries) or global linearity across regimes (off-diagonal entries). Full details of
models and datasets are in Section 3.3.

Vision embeddings exhibit locally linear structure. Table 1 (second column) shows that 99.1%
of all (model, concept, regime) triplets (over a total of 2,530 tests) yield local DC scores statistically
above baseline (per-matrix Bonferroni-corrected Welch’s t-test), with the lowest rate being 97.8% for
dry→wet. In particular, from the representative results in Figure 2, diagonal entries (local linearities)
in the DC matrices are consistently above zero across models and concepts. Even for a supervised
ViT (leftmost column), DCMNIST
                            2→3     = 0.45 and DCroom clean→dirty = 0.52, significant against near-zero
random baselines. We conclude that statistically detectable (but not perfect) local linearity is almost
universal across models, concepts and regimes.

Linearity is often fragmented across regimes. From Table 1 (third column), only 52.2% of
all (model, concept, regime-pair) tuples yield cross-regime DC above baseline (over 13,984 tests),
compared to 99.1% for local linearity. We show qualitative breakdowns across regime-pairs in
Figure 2. For the supervised ViT on numerosity (2→3), in 8 of 12 regime pairs, cross-regime DC is
indistinguishable from the random baseline (fragmentation = 0.67), despite significant local DCs.
On the other hand, CLIP achieves fragmentation = 0 for the same transition: all cross-regime DCs
are significant. Similarly, for cleanliness, supervised ViT has fragmentation 0.92 (11/12 cross-regime
pairs near baseline), while CLIP has fragmentation 0.08 (only one insignificant pair). We conclude
that regime-specific linearity can coexist with near-absent global linearity.

(94.6%) of all statistical significance tests passing respectively. Intuitively, for objects and age,
regimes differ in style (photo vs. sketch, face vs. hand) while the visual cues for the concept (shape
for objects, wrinkles for age) remain consistent. For more abstract concepts like numerosity and

Our findings above indicate that concept representations in vision embeddings can be significantly
locally linear yet fully fragmented across regimes (Figure 2, left). While we have so far treated
linearity as binary (either statistically significant or not) we now ask whether global linearity varies
across models of different local linearities. Does stronger within-regime linear structure in modern
foundation models reflect deeper concept understanding, or simply stronger regime-specific biases
(leading to even weaker global understanding)?


Global linearity increases with local linearity. From Table 1, the rank correlation, measured
by Kendall τ , between global and local linearity across the tested models is positive for every
concept (τ ∈ [0.38, 0.89], all p < 10−3 ). As qualitative examples, Figure 3 plots global linearity
(Equation (6)) and fragmentation (Equation (7)) against local linearity (Equation (4)) across all
models, for representative concept transitions (2→3, clean→dirty, foggy→sunny). Global linearity
correlates positively with local linearity in all three cases (τ = 0.68, 0.59, 0.77). This finding
echoes the platonic representation hypothesis [44]: stronger local concept structure in vision models
converges toward abstract, regime-invariant geometry. Crucially, we do not claim a causal effect; our
result describes the trend that is emerging in currently popular vision foundation models.

5   Conclusion
Almost all vision models encode “three”, but not all of them know it is “three”. While vision
embeddings are locally linear to the extent of statistical significance almost without exception, many
of them learn the same concept several times, once per regime, reflected in the phenomenon we
characterize as fragmentation. As locally linear structure gets stronger, cross-regime alignment
increases and global linearity emerges, producing semantically meaningful concept directions that
can act as effective linear probes. Given our characterization of vision embedding geometry, the
overarching question as to whether regime-specific skills are also converging into abstract ones (and
whether they are related to geometry) remains open to future work.

Limitations and broader impact. Although we cover a broad range of concepts exhibiting varying
degrees of difficulty, our conclusions are limited in scope to the investigated concept types. Our
counterfactual pairs are approximate: matched by embedding space distance. While these limitations
are unavoidable in the current scope, we argue that our statistical tests and downstream capability
(probing) results together establish sufficient evidence to validate our main claims.
Linear directions in embedding space can potentially be exploited to steer representations toward
harmful outputs; our work characterizes this geometry but does not introduce new attack surfaces
beyond what already exists in existing models. On the other hand, our work contributes to the
literature on interpretability, which strives to make AI systems fair and transparent.
