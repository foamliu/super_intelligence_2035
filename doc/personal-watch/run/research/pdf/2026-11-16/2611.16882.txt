Abstract
How does data shape language model (LM) behavior throughout pretraining? We
investigate this question through a case study on entity comparison, e.g., Between
France and Brazil, which country is larger?, across nine comparison tasks spanning
five entity types. We begin with controlled experiments in which we train small
LMs (163M parameters) on mixtures of natural text from pretraining corpora and
synthetic task data. Our analysis identifies three distinct phases of learning: (1) an
early phase where the LM selects entities by frequency, (2) a middle phase where
the LM selects entities by position in the prompt (first vs. last mentioned entity),
and (3) a late phase where the LM selects the entity that is the correct answer. We
show that the emergence of these three phases is causally determined by statistical
properties of the training data. With small amounts of task-specific synthetic data,
we observe only the first two phases and the model fails to learn the task; with
large amounts, the model jumps directly from the frequency-based heuristic to
solving the task correctly. Moreover, changing the entity frequency distribution
from a naturally occurring Zipfian distribution (a small number of entities are very
common and the vast majority are rare) to a uniform one weakens the frequency
phase and speeds up learning of the task. Lastly, we show that the three phases of
training dynamics from our controlled experiments appear in the open source model
OLMo-2 32B. Together, our findings demonstrate that properties of pretraining
data are causal drivers of heuristic learning and show that small-scale synthetic
experiments can predict training dynamics at larger scales.1

Introduction

of data drive these learning dynamics? The relationship between data and model has proven fertile
ground for interpretability research, with recent works uncovering how properties of data drive the
formation of structured internal representations (Merullo et al., 2025; Karkada et al., 2026; Prieto
et al., 2026), algorithmic motifs (Olsson et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025), generalization
capabilities (Chan et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024; Ruis et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), and imperfect

dynamics in which we study how LMs learn to make factual comparisons between entities, e.g.,
Between France and Brazil, which country is larger?
Answering such questions properly requires retrieving the relevant scalar attributes for each entity
(e.g., the area of a country), and performing a numerical comparison between those scalar values
(Elazar et al., 2019). However, models can instead rely on simpler heuristics, such as the answer’s
position in the prompt (Zheng et al., 2023), or how often the answer is mentioned in the training
data (Gottesman et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Thus, this task provides a natural testbed for
understanding (1) when different strategies emerge and disappear throughout training and (2) how
entity frequency in pretraining data shapes such learning dynamics.
To answer the first question, we characterize model behavior throughout training, using three pieces
of information: the difference in scalar attributes for each entity pair (the correct algorithm), entity
frequency, and positional bias. We introduce a controlled pretraining setup in which we train GPT2-scale models (163M parameters) from scratch. At each training checkpoint, we use a regression
model to identify which features best predict model outputs, allowing us to track the evolution of
strategies over time. Our analysis reveals three distinct phases of learning. First, the LM develops a
frequency-based heuristic, outputting the entity that has been seen more often in training thus far.
Next, a position-based heuristic takes over where the LM outputs either the first- or last-mentioned
entity. Finally, the LM learns the correct comparison algorithm (see Figure 1).
We then investigate how properties of the pretraining data drive these learning dynamics (Section 3).
To make the small LM learn the comparison behavior, we supplement natural text from the pretraining
corpus with synthetic data, which contains examples of correctly solving the task. With insufficient
synthetic task data, the LM only goes through the first two phases, developing a frequency heuristic
then a position heuristic with no further learning. Increasing the proportion of task data first
strengthens positional bias and would more quickly drive the LM to learn the comparison algorithm.
In a second experiment, we vary the distribution of entity frequencies. Naturally occurring text follows
a Zipfian distribution (Condon, 1928), where a small number of entities are very high frequency while
the vast majority of entities are very low frequency; e.g., in the OLMo-2 pretraining corpus, China
appears 441M times whereas Luxembourg appears only 6.8M times. We show that we can shorten the

duration of the first two phases and temper the strength of the frequency heuristic by using uniform
distributions for the synthetic or natural pretraining data.
Lastly, we then ask whether the same phase structure arises in larger models pretrained on natural
text alone, by analyzing checkpoints from the OLMo-2 family (1B, 7B, 13B, and 32B parameters)
across all nine tasks (Section 4). We find the same phases arising in the same order, although which
phases appear at all varies with task and model scale. This suggests that the observed dynamics are
not an artifact of the synthetic setup; the resulting phase structure also characterizes pretraining at
scale. Together, our results identify properties of pretraining data as causal drivers of the strategies
LMs adopt, showing that they can control which strategies emerge during training and when.

2

Setup

Task formulation. The entity comparison task is defined by triples (d, e1 , e2 ), where e1 and e2
are entities (e.g., countries) and d is a domain corresponding to a numerical property (e.g., area).
Each entity-domain pair (d, e) is associated with a scalar value vd,e . The algorithm for solving a
comparison query A(d, e1 , e2 ) = max(vd,e1 , vd,e2 ) returns the entity with the higher value. We study
nine comparison tasks spanning five entity types: countries, U.S. states, U.S. universities, movies,
and notable people. Each comparison task has two directions, where the correct answer is either the
larger or the smaller entity. We perform pretraining on GPT-2-scale models using three tasks (country
area, country GDP, and people birth year), and evaluate OLMo-2 models on all tasks. The full list of
tasks is provided in Appendix A.
Training data. We use subsets of the fully open-source OLMo-2 pretraining corpus,
OLMo-mix-1124 (OLMo et al., 2024), which is used primarily for stage-1 pretraining of OLMo-2
models. Entity mention frequencies in this corpus follow a heavy-tailed Zipfian distribution, which
Figure 2(a) demonstrates for the country entity. At the 163M scale, models cannot reliably learn
long-tail factual knowledge from natural text alone (Allen-Zhu and Li, 2023; Kandpal et al., 2023),
which we also observed in preliminary experiments. We therefore add synthetic task supervision
data as scaffolding: comparison sentences that exactly match the evaluation template, such as “By
total area, among the two countries A and B, the larger country is”. This synthetic data serves
as a proxy for the task-relevant signal that larger LMs absorb implicitly from massive pretraining
corpora. We use the full set of entities in both training and evaluation. This mirrors natural pretraining,
where models are exposed to all entities they are later evaluated on, but it also means the LM could
memorize the comparisons. To test this, we run an additional experiment with 20% of entity pairs
held out from the synthetic data in Appendix B.5.
Model. We train transformer language models with the GPT-2 architecture (12 layers, 12 heads,
768-dim embeddings) (Radford et al., 2019) from scratch on each data mixture. Training data is
tokenized with the allenai/dolma2-tokenizer, matching the tokenizer used by the OLMo-2
models. This ensures token-level frequency counts are directly comparable across models. We train
each model for two epochs with three random seeds.
Evaluation. We extract the teacher-forced logits of the first generated token at which the entities
differ to determine the model’s choice. We then denote it as a binary output distribution PLM over
the two entities. We measure per-pair accuracy at every saved checkpoint.
2.1

Counting Entity Occurrences in Pretraining Data

To compute frequency-based features, we track cumulative occurrence and co-occurrence counts of
the entity terms up to every saved model checkpoint. This accurately reflects how much exposure
the model has had to each entity at each point during training. For entities with multiple name
variants (e.g. U.S.A., United States, the U.S.), we aggregate counts across all aliases. We adapt the
BatchSearch method from Merullo et al. (2025). We define countt (e) as the number of times
entity e appears in the pretraining corpus up to training step t, and similarly countt (e1 , e2 ) for
co-occurrences. Our hypothesis is that entity frequency in the pretraining corpus influences which
heuristics a model adopts and the order in which they appear during training. Specifically, given a
query (d, e1 , e2 ), if countt (e1 ) > countt (e2 ) at step t, the model can be more likely to predict e1
regardless of whether vd,e1 > vd,e2 .

Natural pretraining corpora exhibit heavy-tailed Zipfian entity frequency distributions. In

synthetic and natural data to study how this naturally occurring distribution affects learning dynamics.
2.2

Three Phases of Learning

To identify which strategies drive LM predictions at each checkpoint during training, we fit a logistic
regression model to predict LM outputs from a set of handpicked features, each corresponding to
a heuristic or to the correct algorithm. By comparing regression accuracy across different feature
subsets, we can track which strategies are predictive at each checkpoint. In preliminary experiments,
we evaluated a wide range of features (full list in Appendix B.1). We found that the best fit uses only
three: the frequency, position, and true value of entities. We describe each below, in the order we
expect them to dominate over the course of training.
1. Frequency feature: We use the log ratio of cumulative occurrences of e1 and e2 in the
training corpus up to step t: log (countt (e1 )/countt (e2 )). A model relying on this feature
is using a popularity heuristic, predicting whichever entity it has seen more often. More
broadly, we expect this feature to dominate model predictions early in training, before the
model has had sufficient exposure to relevant entities.
2. Positional bias feature: Since the two entities are mentioned sequentially, each can appear
in either the first or last position. A model in mid-training may be biased to always output
the entity in one of these positions, regardless of where the correct answer is. Since the
task is a binary choice, we formalize this bias using the regression intercept, which absorbs
any systematic preference for the first- or last-mentioned entity. Crucially, every entity pair
is evaluated in both orderings, so any systematic entity-specific bias, such as frequency,
scalar value, or tokenization, is counterbalanced across positions. This ensures that the
intercept isolates the model’s residual preference for a prompt position (see Appendix B.2
for verification).
3. Value feature: We use the log ratio of the true values of e1 and e2 in the question domain d:
log (vd,e1 /vd,e2 ). This is the only one of the three features predictive of the ground truth
answer, so a model relying on it has learned the underlying task. We expect this feature to
emerge late in training.

3

Learning Dynamics in Small LMs with Synthetic Data

To study how heuristics develop during training, we start with training small language models from
scratch on mixtures of natural text from the OLMo-2 pretraining corpus and synthetic data from
the entity comparison task. First, we vary the amount of synthetic task data relative to natural text
and find that, with the right balance of data, three phases of learning emerge: a frequency heuristic
first, then a position heuristic, and finally the correct algorithm (Section 3.1). Then, we change the
entity distributions in the training data from Zipfian to uniform and observe how this affects the
development of these heuristics (Section 3.2).
3.1

How the Ratio of Synthetic Data Shapes the Three Phases

Preliminary experiments demonstrated that the 163M-parameter LMs do not learn the comparison
task when trained on only natural data, which is unsurprising at this scale. To help the model learn
the behavior we want to study, we supplement natural text in the pretraining corpora with synthetic
task-specific data. For the country area task, it follows the form “By total area, among the two
countries A and B, the larger country is ”. Under a fixed total data budget of 1.1B tokens, we vary the
proportion of synthetic task supervision in 0%, 0.01%, 0.1%, and 1%. We generate synthetic data that
matches the Zipfian distribution so that it better mirrors naturally occurring text. The fully natural
setting serves as a control, which allows us to observe the effect of natural text on model behavior.

The proportion of task-specific synthetic data does not affect the frequency phase. Figure 3
shows that the frequency phase is essentially identical across all four conditions: it peaks at step
200 and decays by step 1K, regardless of synthetic ratio. This motivates the experiments in the next
section (§3.2), where we show that the frequency phase depends on the entity frequency distribution
of the training data, and that flattening that distribution weakens the phase.
The correct algorithm emerges only above a critical density of task specific synthetic data. The
difference across conditions is whether the model ever reaches the correct algorithm. At 0% and
0.01% synthetic data, the correct-algorithm feature stays near zero throughout training. At 0.1%, it
emerges around 2.5K steps and rises steadily. When task data is increased to 1%, it emerges within
the first 1K steps and reaches a drop-one accuracy of 18 by end of training. This corroborates existing
work and suggests that there is a threshold: below a critical density of task-relevant signal, the model
never finds learning the task loss-reducing enough to override existing shortcuts (Gu et al., 2025;
Verma et al., 2025).
The position phase shrinks and eventually disappears as task-specific synthetic data increases.
When the correct algorithm phase does not emerge (0% and 0.01%), model behavior converges to a
strong positional bias that persists throughout the rest of training. At 0.1%, a shorter position phase
still appears between the frequency and correct-algorithm phases. At 1%, the position phase is absent
as the model jumps directly from frequency to solving the task correctly.
3.2

How Entity Frequency Distribution Drives the Heuristic Phases

With 0.1% of synthetic task data, we observe the three-phase learning dynamics emerge in training.
However, the previous experiments use synthetic data with entity frequency distributions that match
the Zipfian distribution of naturally occurring data. Now, we separately manipulate the entity
frequency distribution and change from Zipfian to uniform. We run this experiment on three tasks,
country area and country GDP comparison presented in the main paper, and person birth year in
Appendix C.2. For each task, we train a separate model for each of the four natural vs. synthetic
distribution combinations. To construct the uniform setting, we sample sequences that contain country
or people mentions to achieve roughly uniform mentions per entity.
The Zipfian distribution of natural text drives the initial frequency phase. Figure 4 shows the
learning trajectories for country area (left) and country GDP (right). In each block, the columns vary
the entity distribution of the natural text (Zipfian or uniform), and the rows vary the distribution of
the synthetic data. When natural text is Zipfian (first column of each block), both tasks show a strong
early frequency phase, regardless of the synthetic distribution. When natural text is uniform, this
phase is much weaker, and it nearly disappears when both natural text and synthetic data are uniform.

4

Learning Dynamics in OLMo Pretraining

The controlled experiments in Section 3 establish that the Zipfian distribution of entities in text drives
the frequency heuristic phase, and that training on such corpora follows a three-phase structure. We
now ask whether the same pattern appears in models trained at scale on natural text alone, without
any synthetic task supervision.
Setup. We focus on the OLMo-2 model family (1B, 7B, 13B, and 32B) (Groeneveld et al., 2024),
which provides fully open-sourced training data and checkpoints. We apply the same regression
analysis as described in Section 2 to track which strategy dominates over time. We evaluate 26
checkpoints per model, focusing primarily on stage 1 pretraining, with dense early coverage (every 1K
steps from 1K to 10K) and sparser, log-spaced checkpoints afterward. To account for prompt variation,
we evaluate each checkpoint with 3 prompt templates and report mean and standard deviation across
them. The checkpoints and prompts used are presented in Appendix D. We structure this analysis in
two parts. First, we track the country area task across all four model sizes to observe how learning
dynamics change with scale (Figure 6). Since the smaller models do not reliably learn the task, we
then focus on the 32B model and extend the analysis to all nine comparison tasks, in both directions,
to test how well the phase structure generalizes (Figure 7).
4.1

Phase Structure Across Model Scales

Figure 6, all four models exhibit the early heuristic phases observed in our controlled experiments,
and they emerge in the order of frequency followed by position. The frequency heuristic appears
briefly in the earliest checkpoint of all models. The 1B and 7B models then remain in a prolonged
position heuristic phase without learning the task; the 13B model transitions to the correct algorithm
at around 1T tokens, while the 32B model does so earlier and achieves a higher peak. We notice
that the frequency phase has a lower peak than in the small LMs we pretrained, and hypothesize
that this is because the first available checkpoint is already several billion tokens into training. A

4.2

Generalization Across Nine Comparison Tasks

To test how broadly the phase structure generalizes, we extend the analysis to all nine comparison
tasks, in both the larger- and smaller-magnitude directions (18 task variants in total). We focus on
OLMo-2 32B, since early experiments show that the smaller models (especially 1B and 7B) do not
reliably learn the tasks by the end of stage 1 pretraining.
The frequency heuristic dominates at the earliest checkpoint for nearly every task. Figure 7
shows the dominant phase at four selected checkpoints: the first available checkpoint (9B tokens
seen), two intermediate checkpoints, and the final checkpoint (5000B tokens seen) at the end of
stage 1 pretraining. Full results for all checkpoints are in Appendix D.2. At the earliest checkpoint
(9B tokens), frequency is the dominant phase in 15 of the 18 task variants. This includes 8 of the
9 smaller-magnitude variants, where selecting the more frequent entity is typically incorrect. This
suggests that the early phase reflects entity frequency statistics rather than partial task knowledge.
Phases can be skipped, but their ordering is largely preserved. Which phases appear varies by
task, since for some tasks even the 32B model struggles to learn the correct algorithm. However, the
relative order of the three phases is more consistent: across the training trajectory, whenever two
phases both become dominant, they largely follow the frequency, position, value order.

5

Discussion

Implications on training. Across scales from 163M to 32B parameters, models exhibit the same
heuristic phases, and the 1B and 7B OLMo models never escape the positional heuristic. These
heuristics appear to be a structural feature of how the task is learned, rather than an artifact of small or
synthetic data. The key difference is whether the model successfully transitions out of these heuristics,
not whether the heuristics emerge. In controlled experiments, the correct algorithm fails to emerge
below a critical density of task supervision; in OLMo, it fails to emerge below a critical model scale
on natural text alone. This is in line with prior work sugesting that the correct strategy only becomes
loss reducing once the model has sufficient task-relevant signal relative to its capacity. Practically, our
results indicate that the long tail in natural pretraining corpora may impose a real cost on downstream
task acquisition. Flattening these heavy-tailed distributions in pretraining corpora can make learning
more efficient.
Understanding of heuristics learning. We observe three phases in learning: first a frequency
heuristic, then a position heuristic, and finally the correct algorithm. The consistency of this ordering
across very different settings suggests that it reflects a structural property of the task: each phase
corresponds to the easiest-to-learn predictive feature given the model’s current state. Frequency is
directly available from token-level statistics, position depends on syntactic patterns in the data, and
value-based entity comparison requires manipulating factual knowledge. Each subsequent strategy is
likely more expensive to learn, but eventually becomes more loss reducing than its predecessor.
Limitations. We focus our experiments on the task of entity comparison, and the observed phase
structure may not generalize to tasks with different structures. Our controlled experiments are
conducted at the 163M scale in order to efficiently vary data composition and perform model
pretraining under feasible compute budgets, which would otherwise be infeasible for large-scale
pretraining. Although these intervention experiments are small-scale, we show that the toy setting
can nevertheless provide insight into the training dynamics of much larger models, which is itself an
interesting finding. While our OLMo-2 analysis suggests that the same phases emerge at much larger
scales, we cannot establish causal evidence for the role of data at scale.
Lastly, our experiments that modify pretraining data statistics (e.g., changing the country distribution
from Zipfian to uniform) require re-selecting sequences containing the target entities. For example,
this may involve excluding sequences heavily focused on United States politics while selecting
sequences about tropical fruits in Madagascar. This process could introduce domain shifts and other
9

confounding factors that we do not account for. There is currently no straightforward solution to this
issue, and it remains an active open research problem (Nadkarni et al., 2025).
Future work. We would like to understand the underlying mechanisms for each heuristic and more
fully trace the origins of the three phases in this order. For example, whether the positional bias relies
on induction-head-style in-context retrieval and if it relates to when induction heads are formed, and
whether there exists latent representations that track entity frequency.

6

Related Work

Heuristics and different task strategies. LMs often rely on heuristics rather than the intended
algorithms for solving tasks. Prior work has identified shortcuts in natural language inference (McCoy
et al., 2019), arithmetic reasoning (Razeghi et al., 2022; Nikankin et al., 2025), multi-hop reasoning (Yang et al., 2025), and factual recall, where entity popularity sharply predicts whether a model
answers correctly (Mallen et al., 2023; Kandpal et al., 2023). Most closely related to our setting,
Lehmann et al. (2026) study entity comparison in fully trained LLMs and identify three heuristic
biases that influence predictions. A complementary view characterizes LM behavior as a mixture
of strategies rather than a single mechanism (Wu et al., 2025; Gur-Arieh et al., 2025; Pîslar et al.,
2025; Todd et al., 2025). These analyses operate on static checkpoints; we instead study the learning
trajectory itself, asking when each heuristic emerges during training and what data properties drive
its emergence.
Pretraining data. Pretraining data affects model behavior, performance, and internal representations (Elazar et al., 2022; McCoy et al., 2023; Shaib et al., 2024; Merullo et al., 2025; Ruis et al., 2025;
Verma et al., 2025). Wang et al. (2025) measure the correlation between LLM output probabilities
and the frequency of task-relevant n-gram pairs in pretraining data, while other work has traced
model biases and failures to asymmetries in entity frequency (Razeghi et al., 2022; He et al., 2025;
Kandpal et al., 2023; Mallen et al., 2023). To make causal claims about data effects, prior work has
intervened directly on training data composition (Wei et al., 2021; Chan et al., 2022; Allen-Zhu and
Li, 2024); Nadkarni et al. (2025) provide a recipe for document-level interventions, and Longpre et al.
(2024) systematically study effects of data age, domain coverage, and quality. Our work builds on
this interventional methodology and identifies entity distribution as a causal driver of which heuristic
phases emerge during training.
Phases and training dynamics. Capabilities are often acquired during pretraining through abrupt
transitions or distinct phases that build on earlier representations (Saphra and Lopez, 2019; Chen
et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). A canonical case study is the formation of induction heads, which
coincides with a sharp drop in loss and the emergence of in-context learning (Olsson et al., 2022;
Singh et al., 2024). Other work tracks how the internal mechanisms behind a capability change over
training: the attention heads implementing a circuit can be replaced over time while the algorithm
they implement stays the same (Tigges et al., 2024), and components can shift from general-purpose
to task-specific as a model gets better at predicting an answer (Hakimi et al., 2025). Closest to our
setting, Zucchet et al. (2025) also find that factual recall emerges in distinct phases, with a plateau
before precise recall that shortens under more balanced training data; Chang et al. (2024) similarly
study how factual knowledge is acquired and forgotten over the course of pretraining. Ravfogel et al.
(2025) trace the emergence of a linear truth direction, and Gu et al. (2025) study phase transitions as a
function of data mixing ratio. Our work extends this perspective to natural pretraining data statistics.

7

Conclusion

We identify three phases of learning in entity comparison and show through controlled experiments
that pretraining data composition causally determines which phases emerge and when. The same
phase structure appears in OLMo-2 models trained on natural text at scales up to 32B parameters,
suggesting these dynamics are a general feature of how LMs learn this task. Our results highlight
pretraining data statistics as both a driver of heuristic learning and a controllable lever for studying it.

Feature Ablation Experiments

B.1

List of Evaluated Features

To identify the strategies that LMs use for the entity comparison task, we evaluate a wide range
of features in initial experiments. For each input triple (d, e1 , e2 ), we construct a feature vector
ϕ(d, e1 , e2 ) to capture relevant features that correspond to possible heuristics. We consider four
families of features:
1. Positional bias feature: binary indicators of left/right position, independent of entity
semantics or values (e.g., whether e1 is favored by virtue of being mentioned first). This is
the intercept in the logistic regression model.
2. Frequency-based features: entity occurrence and co-occurrence counts in the pretraining
corpus (e.g., sum of the occurrences of e1 and e2 ).
3. Value-based features: variants of the numerical values vd,e1 and vd,e2 , including absolute
values, log-scaled values, differences, and ratios.
4. Related-domain features: values of numerical properties other than d that may co-vary
with vd,e (e.g., population or GDP when d is area), intended to test whether models rely on
these correlated proxies.
For the frequency and domain values, we include normalized variants (e.g., z-scores, distance-tomean) to test if the model responds to relative magnitude rather than absolute values. The full list of
features and their corresponding definitions is provided in Table 2.

Validating Positional Bias Feature

Because every entity pair is evaluated in both prompt orders (Section 2), we can classify each
unordered pair into one of four categories: the model chooses whichever entity appears first, regardless
of which entity that is; the model chooses whichever entity appears second; the model chooses the
correct entity in both orderings; or the model chooses the incorrect entity in both orderings. A
strong position drop-one accuracy should correspond to a large share of pairs falling into the first
two categories, while a stable entity-specific bias (e.g., from frequency, value, or tokenization) would
instead fall into the last two categories. Table 3 reports this breakdown for task and checkpoint

C

GPT-2 Pretraining Experiments

The GPT-2-scale small LMs are trained on 4 NVIDIA H100 GPUs with bfloat16 mixed precision,
which takes roughly two hours for 7,560 training steps. Each model is trained for two epochs over a
mixture of about 1B tokens. We save and evaluate a checkpoint every 200 steps.
Each model is trained with three random seeds (3407, 42, 123), which set the initialization and the
order of the data. We use AdamW (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, weight decay 0.1) with a learning rate that
warms up linearly over 800 steps. Gradients are clipped to a norm of 1.0. The effective batch size is
256 sequences of 1024 tokens (16 sequences per GPU × 4 GPUs × 4 gradient-accumulation steps).
Each training sequence is of 1024 tokens.
C.1

Training Data

Here, we describe the data compositions for training the small LMs in each controlled setup.
Varying the amount of synthetic data. For experiments in Section 3.1, we hold the data distributions of natural and synthetic text fixed to Zipfian (matching the OLMo-mix-1124 distribution) and
vary only the ratio of natural to synthetic text. To reach a target amount of synthetic data, we tile
the synthetic comparison statements until they sum to the desired token count. We fill the rest of the
token budget with natural text data sampled from OLMo-mix-1124. The exact token splits used in
each run are listed in Table 4.
Varying the distribution. For experiments in Section 3.2, we hold the ratio of natural to synthetic
text fixed to 0.1% and vary the entity frequency distributions of either natural text or synthetic data,
one at a time. The distribution can be either Zipfian or uniform, which yields four conditions. Table 5
reports summary statistics of the resulting combined distributions, and Figure 17 visualizes the
distribution in each condition.
